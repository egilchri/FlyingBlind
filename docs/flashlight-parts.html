Putting it together

  1. Load the firmware onto the board first (see below), while it's easy to reach.
  2. Push the wires onto the board's pins as in the table below.
  3. Fit the sensor behind the window at the front of the head, lenses facing out. Then fit the board on the floor of the head behind it, and the buzzer module under the grille.
  4. Plug the switch cable into the board, fold the spare cable into the neck, and push its button through the hole on top of the handle.
  5. Slide the power bank into the handle from the tail, plug it in, and put the tail cap on.
  6. Click the button. You should hear a short chirp, then a tone as soon as the sensor sees something.

Wiring

Seven push-on wires, plus the USB cable. The XIAO's pins are labelled on the board, so find each one by name. The Y splitter joins the two ground wires onto the board's single GND pin.

Before switching on

Check which wire is which on the sensor's cable, using the labels on the sensor or the seller's diagram. Don't rely on wire colors. Power on the wrong pin can damage the sensor. The sensor pin numbers come from a vendor's notes, not Benewake's official datasheet. Also check that the buzzer module is passive and marked S, + and −. Some are labelled I/O, VCC and GND instead.

Loading the firmware

  1. Install the free Arduino IDE on the Mac, then add ESP32 support: Boards Manager → esp32 by Espressif. Choose XIAO_ESP32S3 under Tools → Board.
  2. Open firmware/lidar-ble/lidar-ble.ino from the FlyingBlind repo. It still needs the XIAO pin settings (D7 and D6 for the sensor, D0 for the buzzer). That's a small change, to be made before the parts arrive.
  3. Plug the board into the Mac with a normal USB-C cable that carries data, and click Upload. The XIAO needs no extra driver on a Mac.
  4. The scanner works on its own. The phone app can still connect to it over Bluetooth as ESP32 LiDAR if you want speech as well.
